Synthesis via Harmonic Based Reinforcement Learning

Abstract—Buffer insertion is a critical technique for delay
reduction, and in particular the construction of Steiner trees with
buffers has garnered great attention in recent years. However,
constructing Steiner tree and buffer insertion are often addressed
as separate tasks, due to the large computational complexity for
integrating them concurrently. Considering the interdependence
between tree topology and buffer placement, it should gain
significant benefit for circuit design if we can combine them
jointly in the optimization. In this paper, we introduce a novel
reinforcement learning-based approach to simultaneously achieve
the construction of Steiner trees and buffer insertion, where
the key idea is employing an appropriately designed harmonic
function in the optimization. We also conduct a set of experiments
to validate the efficiency and effectiveness of the proposed method
in depth.
Index Terms —Steiner tree, Elmore delay, buffer, simple har-
monic function.
I. I NTRODUCTION
As the VLSI industry continues to advance, the feature
size of integrated devices has significantly decreased, and the
issue of interconnect delay becomes one of the predominant
factors that should be considered in chip design [1]. To
mitigate this delay issue, buffer insertion has emerged as an
increasingly common technique [2, 3]. A buffer is typically
a gate consisting of two serially-connected inverters, which
regenerate a signal without altering its functionality [1]. In
advanced technology nodes, buffers constitute more than 30%
of cells in VLSI circuits [4], and this proportion reaches even
an alarming 70% at the 32-nm technology node [5]. As a
consequence, judicious buffer insertion plays a key role in
designing circuits. As demonstrated in Fig. 1(a) and Fig. 1(b),
inserting a buffer can help manage signal integrity and timing,
thereby leading to decreased delay. The “placement” and
“quantity” of buffers are two critical factors for constructing an
ideal circuit. Improper placement or excessive use of buffers,
as shown in Fig. 1(c) and Fig. 1(d), can even increase the
delay. Additionally, it has been proved that the problem of
finding optimal buffering is NP-complete [6, 7].
Existing methods for buffer insertion often require a pre-
defined tree topology, which can take the form of rectilinear
Steiner tree. A rectilinear Steiner tree is defined as a tree that

edges (i.e., vertical and horizontal line segments) along with
additional auxiliary points (i.e., Steiner points, as illustrated
in Fig. 2(a)). For the sake of simplicity, we will refer to
the rectilinear Steiner tree as “Steiner tree” henceforth. As a
representative example, Van Ginneken [8] proposed a dynamic
programming approach for buffer insertion. This algorithm
utilizes a Steiner tree and a set of “buffer candidates” along
the tree as inputs, providing the final buffer placement as the
output. Subsequent extensions to this algorithm have further
demonstrated its efficiency [9], and the algorithm proposed by
Shi and Li in [10] achieves a time complexity of O(n log2n)
and a space complexity of O(n logn), where n is the number
of nodes in the tree. However, these methods are limited to
determining the optimal positions for buffer insertion, since
the positions can only be selected from the given candidates.
Therefore, the effectiveness of their models is highly depen-
dent on the initially constructed Steiner tree and the chosen
buffer candidates.
To explore a broader optimization space, several studies try
to simultaneously construct a Steiner tree while performing
buffer insertion . For instance, Lillis et al. [11] presented
an algorithm employing dynamic programming to dynami-
cally determine both the tree structure and buffer positions
based on timing constraints, but it suffers from a high time
complexity of O(n5). Subsequent works were proposed with
utilizing more efficient data structures to reduce the compu-
tational cost [12–14]. However, these approaches still cannot
achieve satisfying computational efficiency. For example, the
approaches of [13] and [14] still take relatively high time

complexities, which are O(n3 logn) and O(n2 logn) respec-
tively. Overall, while these algorithms generally yield high-
quality solutions, the expanded joint optimization space leads
to significantly increased computational complexities.
To tackle the high complexity challenge, we can consider
developing a deep learning-based method for this problem,
as the majority of computational cost taken by deep learning
comes from the offline training stage and the testing stage is
usually fast (namely, once the model has been built after the
training stage, we can apply it to solve any given new instance
within a short time). Actually, deep learning has already been
widely applied to various tasks in the area of EDA [15],
especially for the placement and routing problems [16–18].
For instance, Liu et al. [16] proposed a promising method
for constructing Steiner trees using reinforcement learning.
Recently, Liang et al. [19] proposed a transformer-based model
to generate optimized buffer tree topology.
In this paper, we aim to address the issue caused by
the separating Steiner tree construction from buffer insertion.
Specifically, we consider to develop a learning-based approach
to handle both tasks in an integrated way. Central to our
method is the identification of buffer candidates (i.e.,
the predetermined locations where buffers can potentially be
inserted, each offering the option to include a buffer or not).
Together with the prior algorithms (e.g., [10]) that can operate
on Steiner trees with predefined buffer candidates, we pro-
pose a reinforcement learning-based approach for constructing
Steiner trees that incorporates the identification of appropriate
buffer candidates at each step of the construction process.
The highlights of our approach: As discussed before, the
“quantity” and “placement” are two critical factors for buffer-
ing, but both contribute to high variability and combinatorial
complexity. The core part of our approach for resolving this
issue relies on the following idea:
We convert the buffering task, which is a challenging
“discrete” optimization problem, to a “continuous” predicting
problem, which is built upon a trainable parameterized simple
harmonic function.
This harmonic-based parameterization has flexible repre-
sentation capability to handle the complicated scenarios en-
countered in the rectilinear Steiner tree, such as overlapping
edges and varied topologies. More importantly, this continuous
problem can be efficiently solved by using some appropriately
designed neural network with the stochastic gradient descent
method [20]. To the best of our knowledge, this is the
first study to employ reinforcement learning to tackle this
challenging problem of simultaneous Steiner tree construction
and buffering. We have conducted a comparative analysis of
our method against several open-source algorithms, utilizing
both synthetic data and various industrial benchmarks. The
experimental results demonstrate the superior performance of
our method in reducing timing delays, achieving an average
reduction of around 20% compared to the baseline methods.

II. P ROBLEM FORMULATION
Before delving into the formulation of our problem, we
briefly explain the impact of buffers in a circuit. Properly
inserting a set of buffers in a circuit can reduce the capaci-
tance that upstream nodes must drive [9]. Moreover, a lower
capacitance corresponds to a decreased electrical load on these
nodes, leading to faster signal propagation. Therefore, we can
effectively enhance the timing performance of the circuit .
We consider a set of n nodes, denoted as V =
{v0,v 1,...,v n−1}, where v0 serves as the source and the
remaining nodes {v1,...,v n−1} act as sinks. The input pa-
rameters are defined as follows:
• Node: each node vi (0≤ i < n) is characterized by its
coordinates (xi,yi) and its capacitance Ci.
• Buffer: for a buffer b, the resistance, capacitance, and
intrinsic delay are denoted as Rb, Cb, and Kb.
• Wire: the resistance and capacitance per unit length of
the wire are respectively represented by Rw and Cw.
For each sink node vi, we use “ τi” to represent its signal
delay, defined as the propagation time from the source node v0
to vi. We utilize the widely adopted Elmore delay model [1]
to compute this signal delay, which depends on the above
mentioned parameters of buffer and wire delay, as illustrated in
Fig. 2 (we also refer the reader to the textbook [1] for the strict
definition and more details of Elmore delay in mathematics).
Our objective is to construct a Steiner tree incorporating
buffer candidates. Subsequently, we will apply an existing
efficient buffer insertion algorithm, like the algorithm proposed
by Shi and Li [10], to derive the final Steiner tree with
integrated buffers. As we know, the goal of chip design is
to optimize performance, power, and area (PPA) [1]. While
the inserted buffers can effectively reduce signal propagation
delay and enhance the timing performance of the circuit,
they also occupy certain silicon area, which directly affect
layout resources [21]. Additionally, the inclusion of buffers can
contribute to increased power consumption [22]. Therefore, to
achieve a promising PPA, it is crucial to control the number
of inserted buffers. That is, we should minimize the maximum
delay across all sink nodes while avoiding placing too many
buffers, i.e., minimize

III. O UR PROPOSED HARMONIC BASED REINFORCEMENT
LEARNING METHOD
As discussed before, there should be a large space to
improve the performance, if we could conduct a joint opti-
mization on both the tree topology and buffering. Motivated
by such a “global” goal, we employ a Reinforcement Learning
(RL) framework that incorporates the identification of buffer
candidates at each step of the Steiner tree construction. To
realize this purpose, we need to address two fundamental chal-
lenges: how to determine the number of buffer candidates
for each edge? Further, how to determine their locations?
As illustrated in Fig. 1(c) & 1(d), these two issues can
greatly impact the overall delay. Moreover, these two issues
are inherently difficult to solve due to the high variability
and combinatorial complexity , which can also dramatically
complicate the training process of RL. Actually, because an RL
model typically can be optimized using neural networks with
an efficient stochastic gradient descent method, it is natural to
ask the following question:
Is it possible to build a parameterized and differentiable
model for the discrete buffering problem, so as to integrate it
seamlessly into the RL training process?
If we can build such a trainable model, the overall com-
putational efficiency for the joint optimization can be largely
improved. We begin by providing an overview of our frame-
work in Section III-A. The methodology for constructing edges
at each step is outlined in Section III-B. Additionally, the
key insight of our framework, parameterizing the locations
of buffer candidates, is detailed in Section III-C. Finally,
Section III-D discusses the detailed model architectures for
predicting buffer candidates and other essential modules.
A. Overall Reinforcement Learning Framework
In our RL framework, we utilize the classical “Actor-Critic”
architecture [23]. Both the actor and critic modules receive
n nodes, characterized by their coordinates and capacitance,
as the input. The “actor” is tasked with the incremental
construction of the Steiner tree and predicting the buffer
candidates for each edge, as illustrated in Fig. 3. Thus, the
actor’s output consists of the Steiner tree topology together
with the buffer candidates for each edge. After obtaining these
outputs, we address the overlapping edges and finalize the
buffer candidates. Subsequently, we apply a buffer insertion
algorithm, as described in [10], to derive the final Steiner
tree. For each input instance V (as described in Section II),
we represent the corresponding Steiner tree with the inserted
buffers produced by the actor as “ B”, and let the objective
value derived from Eq. 1, be denoted as Obj(V,B). The
“critic” network serves to establish a baseline by predicting
the objective value, where the predicted value is denoted as
Pre(V ). The performance of the actor with B is evaluated
using the advantage value [23]: Pre(V )− Obj(V,B). This
advantage value serves as the reward signal aimed at enhancing
the actor’s performance. Then, we can define the following
loss functions for training our model.

of the training process, we sample N ≥ 1 sets of points,
denoted as V ={V1,...,V N} (where N represents the batch
size and each Vj contains a set of n nodes). For each Vj, we
have the corresponding “Bj” outputted from the actor (similar
to the previously defined B). The loss function for the actor

B. Constructing an Edge at Each Step
Before introducing our method, we present some notations
that will be used throughout this work. To achieve joint
optimization of topology and buffering, the Steiner tree is
constructed incrementally by adding an edge at each step.
This stepwise procedure enables the identification of buffer
candidates during the Steiner tree construction process. Specif-
ically, the nodes are categorized into two sets: visited nodes
and unvisited nodes. At the t-th step ( 1≤ t≤ n− 1), the
visited nodes collectively form a subtree, with one unvisited
node (denoted as ut) selected to connect to a visited node
(denoted as wt), and then ut is labeled as visited.
The task that selecting a couple of unvisited and visited
nodes is analogous to that addressed in “ REST” [16], which
aims to construct a Steiner tree with minimal wire length. We
adopt the same network structure to select ut and wt, but we
need to further consider the buffer information for a joint opti-
mization. The selection is based on the Pointer Network [25],
which is capable of selecting specific components from the
input. This methodology is commonly employed to address
combinatorial problems using learning-based approaches [26].

C. Parameterizing the Location of Buffer Candidates
As we discussed, to facilitate the joint optimization of
topology and buffers, we integrate the identification of buffer
candidates into each step of the Steiner tree construction.
Traditionally, the identification of buffer candidates at each
step involves determining both their number and positions,
which presents a discrete optimization problem. Consequently,
a key difficulty lies in parameterizing this task as a continuous
prediction problem to ensure compatibility with the training
processes of neural networks. It is noteworthy that in the do-
main of chip design, several other tasks have been modeled as
parameterized problems that can be efficiently solved through
differentiable algorithms, such as “DREAMPlace” [27] and

IV. E XPERIMENTS
We trained our model using PyTorch and conducted our
experiments on a 64-bit Linux system equipped with one
NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPU. The models were trained for
degrees ranging from 10 to 50, aligning with the most common

C. Hyperparameter Selection
Recall that there is a regularization coefficient λ in Eq. 1
that penalizes the number of inserted buffers. We investigated
the effects of varying λ on the maximum delay and buffer
count. In Fig. 7, we present the average delay values and
buffer counts of our synthetic datasets for different λ settings.
Upon analyzing the results, we observed that when λ is in
the range of [0.25, 0.5], the reduction in buffer count slows,
and the maximum delay begins to increase significantly. Since
we prioritize the maximum delay objective in this study, we
selected λ = 0.25 for the experiments discussed above.
V. C ONCLUSION
In this paper, we focus on the simultaneous construction
of Steiner trees and buffering. We integrate the identification
of buffer candidates into each step of the Steiner tree con-
struction process. By parameterizing the task of identifying
buffer candidates with simple harmonic functions, we can
substantially reduce the complexity of the prediction process.
The experimental results demonstrate the effectiveness and
efficiency of our method across synthetic datasets and various
industrial benchmarks. Following this work, it is interesting to
consider developing efficient parameterization techniques for
other optimization problems in EDA, such as global routing
and other significant yet challenging tasks.
